Not every company lands where it said.

Management tells you what the quarter will look like. Three months later they tell you what it was. Nobody keeps the score, so nobody knows which teams routinely sandbag, which routinely disappoint, and which just stopped giving you a number at all.

What arrives, and when

An email, minutes after the company files its results. It carries the record — every guide it gave, and where the figure landed against it — and whatever the company guided for next. You already have the release. What you do not have is whether this management habitually lands where it said it would.

Nothing in it is a forecast. It counts; you decide what the count means.

What it refuses to do

Score a full-year guide against a quarter
Macy's guides the year and reports a quarter. Comparing them reads as a two-point miss ten months before the year is over. A guide is only scored against the period it was for.
Mix adjusted with as-reported
Walmart guided operating income growth of 7 to 10 per cent and the release carries two answers: 26.9 per cent against last year as first reported, 17.4 per cent against the adjusted base the guide meant. The second is the honest one.
Take a midpoint
The middle of a range is a number management never gave. A result is reported by where it fell against the range they printed.
Call a spin-off a guidance cut
Honeywell's sales guide halved between two releases. It did not cut its outlook by half; it separated its businesses. Moves that large are named for what they are.
Publish a number it cannot stand behind
Where a gap is too large to take at face value, the comparison is held back and the fact that something was held back is printed.
